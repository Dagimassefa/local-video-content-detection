# Performance measurements

All numbers here were produced by `npm run bench`, which builds the production bundle, serves it over
plain HTTP, drives the real application in a real browser, and reads results out of the live store.
Nothing is mocked and nothing was transcribed by hand. Re-run it and you get your own numbers.

Firefox 153.0 (Gecko), WebKit 26.5 — all three engine families in general use. Full matrix below.

Headed browsers with a real GPU, deliberately. Headless Chrome commonly falls back to SwiftShader for
WebGL and has no WebGPU adapter at all, so benchmarking there would measure a software rasteriser and
report it as GPU performance.

**One machine, no physical phone.** That is the significant gap and it is not closed by anything here.
The production target is mobile and these numbers come from a laptop iGPU. What *has* been done to
approximate it — CPU throttling, WASM inference, a phone viewport, and the engine Safari ships on iOS
— is set out under "Simulated slow devices" below, along with exactly what each does and does not
represent. An Intel Iris Xe is also not a fast GPU by desktop standards, which makes these figures
closer to a mid-range phone than a discrete-GPU desktop would be. That is an argument for
plausibility, not a substitute for measurement.

## Headline results (`auto` backend → WebGPU, `balanced` policy)

| Fixture | Duration | Source | Backend | Model load | Warm-up | **TTFV** | Total | Frames (dup) | Decode p50/p95 | Infer p50/p95 | Coverage | Verdict | Conf | Stop |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `bars-12s.mp4` | 11.9 s | webcodecs | webgpu | 1245 ms | 153 ms | **164 ms** | 334 ms | 2 (0) | 81.6 / 83.1 | 55.1 / 75.8 | 51% | flagged | 0.73 | complete |
| `bars-12s.webm` | 12.0 s | video-element | webgpu | 1116 ms | 137 ms | **2120 ms** | 3701 ms | 31 (19) | 94.8 / 191.7 | 45.3 / 54.9 | 100% | flagged | 0.71 | complete |
| `long-3min.mp4` | 179.2 s | webcodecs | webgpu | 1204 ms | 121 ms | **1048 ms** | 2484 ms | 38 (6) | 15.0 / 37.1 | 49.0 / 60.8 | 75% | clean | 0.86 | complete |
| `scenes-30s.mp4` | 29.9 s | webcodecs | webgpu | 1220 ms | 186 ms | **1211 ms** | 1317 ms | 12 (7) | 52.8 / 94.9 | 48.2 / 57.7 | 97% | clean | 0.95 | complete |
| `static-20s.webm` | 20.0 s | video-element | webgpu | 1083 ms | 168 ms | **1982 ms** | 7702 ms | 63 (4) | 72.5 / 132.4 | 47.1 / 54.0 | 100% | clean | 0.98 | complete |

TTFV = time to first publishable verdict, measured from the moment the model is ready.

### The result that matters most

**Time-to-first-verdict does not scale with duration.** The 179-second video reaches a verdict in
**1048 ms** — faster than the 12-second WebM at 2120 ms, and comparable to the 30-second clip at
1211 ms. TTFV tracks the *frame source's* per-sample cost, not the length of the input, which is
exactly what the fixed-cost survey phase was designed to achieve. A two-hour film would behave the
same way.

The corollary is visible in the coverage column: the long video reports **75%** coverage against the
30-second clip's **97%**. Fewer samples per second of content, and the confidence figure is scaled
accordingly (0.86 versus 0.95) rather than pretending the two scans are equally thorough.

**But TTFV is bounded by per-sample decode cost, and that varies enormously.** A real 720×1280 action
clip of 178.6 s — essentially the same duration as `long-3min.mp4` at 179.2 s — reached its first verdict
in **9,998 ms** rather than 1,048 ms. Nearly 10× slower at the same duration.

The cause is the source, not the length: only 47 keyframes across 179 s, so the file declares a
**3,403 ms temporal resolution**, and heavy inter-frame motion pushes decode p95 to 265 ms against
15 ms for the synthetic clip. Sixteen survey samples at that cost is ten seconds.

This does not contradict the claim, it sharpens it. Time-to-first-verdict is independent of *duration*
and proportional to *per-sample decode cost*. On a phone, where decode is hardware-accelerated but
memory bandwidth is tighter, that is the term to watch — not the length of the video.

### WebCodecs versus `<video>`

Decode p50 on the long MP4 is **15.0 ms** against **52.8–94.8 ms** for the seek-based path — roughly
3.5–6× faster per sample, and it runs entirely off the main thread. The `<video>` path also shows its
seek-granularity limit: 19 of 50 requests on `bars-12s.webm` resolved to a frame already scored.
Those cost a seek but not an inference, since the perceptual cache catches them first.

`bars-12s.mp4` is an instructive outlier: 2 frames and 51% coverage. `MediaRecorder` writes only 3
sync samples in 12 seconds, so the source honestly declares a ~4-second temporal resolution and the
sampler does not ask for more. Reported rather than hidden.

## Backend comparison

Same fixture (`bars-12s.webm`), same policy, backend forced.

| Backend | Model load | Warm-up | Inference p50 | Inference p95 | Notes |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **webgpu** | **1138 ms** | 143 ms | 53.8 ms | 69.0 ms | Default when an adapter is available |
| **webgl** | **15184–16963 ms** | 360–459 ms | **36.9–49.7 ms** | 50.4–65.9 ms | See below |
| **wasm** | 618 ms | 140 ms | 121.9 ms | 221.3 ms | Single-threaded SIMD |

Two findings worth stating plainly.

**WebGL has the fastest steady-state inference and a catastrophic cold start.** Once loaded it matches
or beats WebGPU (36.9 ms vs 53.8 ms p50), but model load is **~15–17 seconds**, reproducibly, across
repeated runs. That is tfjs compiling a shader program per operation across the whole MobileNetV2 graph
through ANGLE → D3D11. It does not amortise away between page loads.

The mobile implication is direct: for a **one-off scan**, cold start dominates and WebGPU wins by more
than ten seconds. For **sustained** scanning of many videos in one session, WebGL is competitive. The
`auto` chain prefers WebGPU, which is the right default for the "user opens a video" case.

This finding also exposed a design flaw. The scan's wall-clock budget originally started before model
load, so a 16-second cold start consumed a 20-second budget and starved the scan to 13 frames with a
misleading `time-budget` stop reason. The clock now starts once the model is ready — model load is a
one-time cost, already reported separately. After the fix the same run completes 31 frames at 100%
coverage.

**WASM is ~2.3× slower than WebGPU**, which is in line with expectations and entirely usable. It is the
floor that makes the fallback chain meaningful rather than theoretical. Single-threaded because
`Cross-Origin-Embedder-Policy` is deliberately not set (it would break cross-origin video URLs — see
[docs/03](03-tradeoffs-and-alternatives.md#8-coep-versus-cross-origin-video)); threads would likely
close much of the gap.

## Main-thread responsiveness

The brief asks that the prototype remain responsive. That is measured rather than asserted, using the
browser's own definition: `PerformanceObserver('longtask')` counts tasks blocking the main thread for
over 50 ms.

| | |
| --- | --- |
| Long tasks during a full scan | **3–6** |
| Worst single task | **81–124 ms** |
| UI frame rate during scanning | **60 fps, sustained** |

Not zero, and worth being precise about where they come from. They cluster at startup — initial React
render, the dynamic tfjs import, backend initialisation — not during the sampling loop. Once scanning
begins the counter stops advancing, because decode and inference are both in workers and frames move
worker-to-worker as transferables without ever touching the UI thread.

For comparison: at ~50 ms of inference plus ~100 ms of decode per sample, running this pipeline on the
main thread would produce roughly one long task per frame, i.e. 30–160 per scan, with visible jank
throughout.

Also measured: `tf.memory()` stays flat at **267 tensors / 10.0 MB** across five back-to-back scans,
which is the loaded model and nothing else. That figure is the leak canary, asserted by
`npm run verify` — and it earned its place, because it initially read
`267 → 534 → 801 → 1068 → 1335`, i.e. the entire model leaking once per scan (see
[docs/03](03-tradeoffs-and-alternatives.md#bugs-the-harnesses-found)).

### What the non-Chromium engines revealed

**Firefox loads the model in 11.6 seconds** against Chrome's 1.15 — a 10× difference on identical
hardware and the same WebGPU backend, and a close parallel to the WebGL cold-start finding below.
Model load, not inference, is the dominant cost on anything but Chromium here. Firefox's inference is
also ~2× slower (94 ms vs 46 ms). Both are arguments for the OPFS caching work in
[docs/05](05-limitations-and-production-path.md), which turns every scan after the first into a warm
start.

Firefox also returns a different verdict on the same clip (clean 0.50 vs flagged 0.73). That is not a
bug — it is the out-of-distribution behaviour described below, where the model's output on synthetic
content is unstable enough that small numerical differences between backends flip a borderline call.
On genuinely in-distribution content this would be far less likely, but it is a real illustration of
why mid-range scores from this model should not be trusted.

**WebKit is the most valuable engine tested despite producing no timings.** Playwright's WebKit build
reports `OffscreenCanvas: false`, `VideoDecoder: false`, `navigator.gpu: false` — the deepest fallback
path in the system — and it ships without media codecs, so it cannot decode the fixtures at all
(`MEDIA_ERR_SRC_NOT_SUPPORTED`). Real Safari has codecs; this build does not.

What the run does establish, and it is not nothing:

- The app **loads and runs** on WebKit. It previously did not: `npm run matrix` failed it outright with
  `Can't find variable: OffscreenCanvas`, an unguarded use in the classifier warm-up that took down the
  entire app before a single frame was classified. **WebKit is the engine Safari ships, including on
  iOS** — half the stated production target — so the app was completely broken on it while working
  perfectly everywhere else.
- Capability detection reports the missing features correctly rather than assuming them.
- It **fails cleanly with a specific error** instead of crashing.

## Simulated slow devices, and proof the governor works

No physical phone was available. CPU throttling via CDP is the honest substitute, with a caveat that
has to be stated because it changes how the numbers should be read:

> `Emulation.setCPUThrottlingRate` slows JS, WASM and the orchestration loop. It does **not** slow the
> GPU. So with a GPU backend, inference and hardware decode run at full desktop speed however hard the
> CPU is throttled — which is exactly what the 4× and 6× rows above show: TTFV rises (200 → 528 ms) as
> the control path slows, while inference p50 does not move.

That also means a GPU-backend throttled run *cannot* exercise the latency governor: the governor watches
inference latency, and inference is not being slowed. Correct behaviour, but no evidence either way.

Putting inference on the **WASM backend** puts it in the throttled path, which is the configuration that
behaves like a genuinely CPU-bound device. Run on `long-3min.mp4`:

| Backend | CPU | Infer p50 | Frames sampled | **Governor** | TTFV | Total | Verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| webgpu (auto) | 1× | 49 ms | **38** | — | 1048 ms | 2484 ms | clean 0.86 |
| **wasm** | 1× | **142 ms** | **31** | **fired ×25** | 2328 ms | 4893 ms | clean 0.84 |
| **wasm** | **6×** | 84 ms | 32 | **fired ×26** | 2305 ms | 3890 ms | clean 0.84 |
| wasm | 1× | 99 ms | 31 | fired ×25 | 1695 ms | 3414 ms | clean 0.84 (mobile viewport) |

This is the evidence the adaptive design needed. With inference at **142 ms against a 45 ms target**,
the governor engages, the frame budget shrinks from 38 to 31, **and a verdict still arrives** with
confidence barely moved (0.86 → 0.84). The system degrades its own thoroughness to fit the device
instead of janking or timing out — previously only asserted by unit tests, now demonstrated end to end
in a real browser.

## Running it on a real handset

The one dimension none of the above covers is physical hardware — a mobile GPU, a mobile decode block,
thermal behaviour. Rather than leave that as a caveat, it is a one-command exercise:

```bash
npm run mobile     # builds, then serves on the LAN and prints the URL
```

Open the printed LAN URL on a phone on the same Wi-Fi, run a scan, and press **Copy as Markdown** in the
Performance card — that produces exactly the row format used in this document, so a real-device row can
be pasted straight into the table above alongside the handset model, OS, browser and resolved backend.

Two things to know before reading such a row:

- **`http://` on a LAN IP is not a secure context**, so WebGPU and Cache Storage are unavailable and the
  app will fall back to WebGL or WASM. That is a realistic *low-tier* measurement and a useful one, but
  it is not the WebGPU path. For that, tunnel the port over HTTPS (`npx localtunnel --port 4200`,
  `cloudflared tunnel`) and use the HTTPS URL.
- **The fallback chain is the thing being tested** in that configuration, which is arguably the more
  important half for a mobile target anyway — it is what a mid-range Android will actually run.

No such row appears above because no handset was available while this was built. The path is provided so
the number is one scan away rather than a rebuild away.

## Spot check on real footage

The fixtures are synthetic, which is why they misbehave. These are real, CORS-enabled, publicly hosted
clips — paste the URL straight into the app. No download, no YouTube, no ToS problem.

| Clip | Content | Verdict | Conf | Peak frame | Coverage |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| [big-buck-bunny](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/bower-media-samples/big-buck-bunny-480p-30s@master/video.mp4) | 3D animation | clean | **0.94** | 0.065 | 100% |
| [face-demographics-walking](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/intel-iot-devkit/sample-videos@master/face-demographics-walking.mp4) | people walking, close-up faces | clean | **0.50** | **0.573** | 100% |
| [people-detection](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/intel-iot-devkit/sample-videos@master/people-detection.mp4) | street CCTV, clothed pedestrians | **flagged** | 0.70 | 0.682 | 100% |
| [store-aisle-detection](https://cdn.jsdelivr.net/gh/intel-iot-devkit/sample-videos@master/store-aisle-detection.mp4) | retail CCTV | clean | **0.93** | 0.078 | 100% |

Three things worth reading off that table.

**Two confident, correct negatives.** Animation and retail CCTV come back clean at 0.93–0.94 with peak
frame scores under 0.08. The model is not simply flagging everything.

**A false positive on ordinary street footage.** `people-detection` is clothed pedestrians on a
pavement, and it flagged at 0.70. That is the documented skin-area bias doing exactly what
[docs/05](05-limitations-and-production-path.md#known-model-biases) says it does. Reported, not hidden.

**The confidence design earning its place.** `face-demographics-walking` peaked at **0.573** against a
0.55 threshold — over the line, but with no second independent frame to corroborate it, so the verdict
stays negative. And confidence lands on **0.50**, the exact floor. That is the maths saying *"the most
suspicious frame I saw was essentially at the threshold, so do not trust this clean verdict"*.

A `max(score)` implementation would have reported 0.57 and told you nothing. A naive one would have
reported "clean, 95% confident". Getting 0.50 here is the system correctly declining to commit.

## Reproducing

```bash
npm install
npm run fixtures                        # ~4 min: records clips in real time
npm run bench                           # every fixture, auto backend
npm run bench -- --backend=wasm         # force a backend
npm run bench -- --only=bars-12s.mp4    # single fixture
npm run verify                          # assert the claims above
HEADLESS=1 npm run bench                # CI: assertions hold, ignore the timings
```

Writes `fixtures/bench-results.json`, `fixtures/bench-results.md`, and a full-page screenshot per
fixture. The in-app perf panel also has a **Copy as Markdown** button producing the same table for a
single interactive run.

## What these numbers do not show

Stated precisely, because the difference between "measured" and "approximated" matters here.

- **No physical mobile device.** This remains the single most important gap. What exists instead:
  a mobile viewport at phone DPR, CPU throttling to 4× and 6×, WASM inference to put the model on a
  CPU-bound path, and WebKit — the engine Safari ships on iOS. Together those cover the *engine* and
  the *CPU* dimensions of the mobile target. They do **not** cover a mobile GPU, a mobile video decode
  block, thermal throttling, or memory pressure. A phone would very likely be slower than any row in
  these tables.
- **No mobile GPU timings.** CDP throttling does not slow the GPU, so every GPU-backend figure here is
  a desktop-GPU figure. The WASM rows are the closest available proxy for a device without a usable
  GPU path.
- **No WebKit timings at all**, because that build has no media codecs. WebKit coverage proves the app
  loads, detects, and degrades correctly there — not how fast it runs.
- **Detection accuracy.** These are latency and throughput figures. There is no precision, recall, or
  ROC number anywhere in this repository, because measuring those requires labelled content that is
  deliberately absent. Reading these numbers as evidence of detection quality would be a misreading.
- **Sustained thermal behaviour.** A five-minute continuous scan on a warm phone is where the governor
  matters most. The governor is now proven to fire under CPU load, but not under thermal decay.
- **One Firefox mobile-viewport cell times out** in a full matrix run while completing in ~12 s when run
  alone. Resource contention from ten sequential browser launches in one process, not an application
  defect — but it is left visible in the output rather than hidden.
